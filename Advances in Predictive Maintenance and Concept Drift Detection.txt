Paper 1: Machine Learning Under Concept Drift
1. Title: Machine Learning Under Concept Drift 1.
2. Authors: Serafeim Tzelepis (Supervisor: Panagiotis D. Tsanakas) 1.
3. Year: 2025 1.
4. Research Problem: Machine learning systems in operational environments face "concept drift" where data properties change over time, leading to silent failure and degraded prediction accuracy without obvious warnings, particularly in safety-critical domains like Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) 2-5.
5. Research Objective: To design, implement, and evaluate a task-agnostic ensemble drift detection architecture with automated calibration that achieves conservative false-positive control while operating across multiple prediction tasks 6-10.
6. Methodology: The study develops a modular, simulation-based platform utilizing a prequential (test-then-train) evaluation protocol, spatial/temporal feature engineering, and a multi-detector ensemble with a consensus mechanism ($3/4$ agreement) to trigger targeted incremental model retraining 11-15.
7. Dataset(s): Floating Car Data (FCD) generated using the SUMO (Simulation of Urban Mobility) traffic simulator for the Athens road network, consisting of 50,000 trips across three distinct 10-hour scenarios (R1 baseline, R2 stable, R3 rain) 11, 16-18.
8. Algorithms: XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Linear Regression; Drift detectors include ADWIN, Page-Hinkley, KSWIN, and Statistical Process Control (SPC) 19-23.
9. Features Used: 25 top features selected via importance ranking, emphasizing route complexity (edge counts, reuse ratio), spatial metrics (K-Means clustering assignments, distances to centroids, target-encoded cluster statistics), and temporal context (hour of day) 24-28.
10. Evaluation Metrics: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Explained Variance ($R^2$), False Positive Rate, and Detection Latency 29-32.
11. Results: The ensemble drift detection achieved zero false positive alarms in stable conditions and accurately detected rain-induced drift within 25 minutes 33-35. Automated incremental retraining reduced the MAE of the trip-stops prediction from 1.149 to 0.898 stops (a 21.9% improvement) 36-38.
12. Strengths: Task-agnostic operation avoiding manual parameter tuning, extreme robustness against false alarms via ensemble consensus, and efficient knowledge retention through incremental gradient-boosting tree adaptation 7, 20, 39-41.
13. Weaknesses: Detection latency of 25 minutes might be excessive for applications requiring rapid response, and the framework relies on simulation data rather than real-world streams 42-44.
14. Limitations: Validation is limited to a single abrupt, weather-induced drift type, computational overhead scales linearly with tasks, and the calibration requires an initial stable observation window 43, 45-47.
15. Future Work: Validation on real-world data, testing across the complete drift taxonomy (gradual, recurring), developing cross-task drift correlation, online continuous calibration, and incorporating predictive uncertainty quantification 48-52.
16. Research Gap: Existing multi-task prediction platforms require heavy manual parameter tuning for each drift detection task and often suffer from unacceptable false alarm rates, lacking automated calibration and complete lifecycle adaptation mechanisms 6, 8, 53, 54.
17. Explainability Method (if any): Uses XGBoost feature importance for ranking inputs during the modeling phase, but does not implement dedicated end-user XAI methods 28, 55.
18. Concept Drift Handling (if any): Monitors smoothed prediction residuals using a combination of 4 statistical detectors (ADWIN, Page-Hinkley, KSWIN, SPC); upon achieving consensus, triggers data collection and adaptive incremental retraining (adding 10-20% estimators to XGBoost) with a reduced learning rate 20, 23, 39, 56.
19. Industrial Application: Urban traffic management, Connected and Cooperative Autonomous Mobility (CCAM), and multi-task Intelligent Transportation Systems 2, 3, 57.
20. Important Figures/Tables: Figure 1.1 (Conceptual Illustration of Drift Detection), Figure 4.1 (Top 10 Feature Importance), Figure 6.1 (Drift Detection timeline), Table 6.1 (Drift Detection Performance Summary), Table 6.2 (Model Adaptation Performance) 32, 37, 55, 58.
Paper 2: Explainable AI for predictive maintenance: A review and standardized evaluation framework
1. Title: Explainable AI for predictive maintenance: A review and standardized evaluation framework 59, 60.
2. Authors: Leila Zemmouchi-Ghomari 59.
3. Year: 2026 (Received/Accepted 2025, Published 2026) 59-61.
4. Research Problem: The complex, opaque nature of black-box AI algorithms in Predictive Maintenance (PdM) limits transparency, operator trust, and the translation of predictions into actionable insights for complex industrial repairs 60, 62, 63.
5. Research Objective: To review recent advancements and challenges of XAI in PdM and introduce the Explainability Parameters (XPA) framework as a structured methodology for evaluating XAI effectiveness 60, 64.
6. Methodology: Systematic literature review synthesizing case studies across industrial sectors, establishing taxonomies of explanations, and proposing a generic multi-phase methodological approach for XAI in PdM 60, 65-68.
7. Dataset(s): Did not introduce a novel dataset; reviewed existing ones like Azure Blob Storage telemetry, NASA FD001 synthetic turbofan data, Petronas gas turbine data, and pump sensor datasets 69-71.
8. Algorithms: Analyzes the application of Random Forests, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Neural Networks, Bayesian models, and transparent models like kNN and Decision Trees 72-74.
9. Features Used: Not applicable directly (review paper), but categorizes pre-modeling methods like feature engineering from time-series and condition data 66, 75.
10. Evaluation Metrics: Standard ML metrics (RMSE, Accuracy, F1-Score, ROC-AUC) paired with XAI-specific evaluation dimensions like 95% Credible Intervals, Chi-factor, CUSUM detection, and the newly proposed XPA framework metrics (Model Fidelity, Stability, Local Accuracy, Coverage, Decomposability) 74, 76, 77.
11. Results: XAI significantly improves trustworthiness and clarifying model predictions; however, implementation is hindered by industrial data complexity and a lack of standardized evaluations. The proposed XPA framework bridges theoretical outputs with actionable maintenance plans 60, 78.
12. Strengths: Comprehensive synthesis of disparate industrial case studies, proposal of a novel structured evaluation metric (XPA), and a focus on actionable learning rather than mere model debugging 60, 64, 76, 79.
13. Weaknesses: The review highlights the reliance on generic/synthetic datasets in the underlying literature and notes computational intensity as an obstacle 74, 80.
14. Limitations: Lacks universally accepted XAI benchmarks in current literature, struggles with establishing causal relationships via post-hoc methods (like SHAP), and identifies socio-economic barriers like digital literacy in adopting XAI 80-83.
15. Future Work: Developing hybrid, context-aware frameworks, prioritizing human-centric designs for knowledge generation, refining explanatory metrics for operational outcomes, and improving models using real-world industrial data 84.
16. Research Gap: Existing XAI methods focus heavily on parameters and model debugging while neglecting outcome/end-user explainability, uncertainty management, and standardized benchmarks for industrial PdM 62, 85, 86.
17. Explainability Method (if any): Analyzes multiple methods: LIME, SHAP, Counterfactual Explanations (CFE), Statistical Distance Metrics, Failure Diagnosis Explainability (FDE), rule extraction, and physics-inspired causal models 74, 87-91.
18. Concept Drift Handling (if any): Not a primary focus, though notes the difficulty in handling dynamic variations and anomalies via continuous anomaly detection (CUSUM) and Deep Learning Uncertainty tracking 77, 92.
19. Industrial Application: Aviation maintenance, nuclear generation assets, gas turbines, automotive wear prediction, sustainable urban water systems, and smart agriculture 82, 90, 93-96.
20. Important Figures/Tables: Figure 1 (Symbolic AI vs Learning-based AI), Figure 4 (High-level ontology of XAI approaches), Table 3 (Case Studies description), Table 5 (Generic XAI Evaluation Framework), Table 6 (Metrics Specific to PdM Application Types) 66, 74, 76, 77, 97.
Paper 3: Predictive Maintenance of Air Pressure System using Boosting Trees: A Machine Learning Approach
1. Title: Predictive Maintenance of Air Pressure System using Boosting Trees: A Machine Learning Approach 98.
2. Authors: Mrugank M Akarte, N. Hemachandra 98.
3. Year: 2018 98, 99.
4. Research Problem: Extreme maintenance costs for Scania trucks are driven by Air Pressure System (APS) breakdowns. The cost of failing to detect a faulty part (False Negative) is 50 times higher than conducting a needless check (False Positive), compounded by a highly skewed dataset 98, 100.
5. Research Objective: To minimize the total maintenance cost of misclassified APS conditions in Scania trucks using a cost-sensitive supervised machine learning approach (gradient boosting trees) to handle data imbalance 98, 101.
6. Methodology: Applied log transformation to reduce data skewness, missing value imputation with median, 5-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning, and a cost-sensitive learning approach using Bayes rule to adjust the probability threshold ($0.0196$) and unequal class weighting 101-105.
7. Dataset(s): APS Failure at Scania Trucks dataset from the UCI Machine Learning repository, consisting of 60,000 training samples and 16,000 test samples, heavily imbalanced at a 1:59 ratio (positive to negative) 100, 101, 103.
8. Algorithms: XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting Trees) 98, 101, 106.
9. Features Used: 171 anonymized attributes derived from heavy truck sensor data (after discarding 7 features that had over 70% missing data) 103, 104.
10. Evaluation Metrics: Total Maintenance Cost (Cost Matrix: $C_1=\$10, C_2=\$500$), Total Normalized Cost, Recall, Precision, F1-score 107-109.
11. Results: The cost-sensitive classifier (Model 1) with unequal class weights heavily minimized false negatives (Recall = 0.968), achieving a total cost of $\$10,140$. A cost-insensitive model (Model 2) cost $\$26,770$ (2.64 times higher). Over-sampling cost $\$29,835$, and under-sampling cost $\$10,720$ 109-111.
12. Strengths: Demonstrates massive cost savings in a heavily imbalanced industrial scenario solely by manipulating loss weighting and decision thresholds without computationally expensive data augmentation techniques 109, 111, 112.
13. Weaknesses: Minimizing false negatives resulted in a significant number of false positives (low precision of 0.4671); features being anonymized restricted domain-specific feature engineering 104, 109.
14. Limitations: Over-sampling risked overfitting and took longer to train; under-sampling risked underfitting by losing valuable majority class data 109, 113.
15. Future Work: Explore other algorithms like random forests, neural networks, ensemble models, advanced sampling techniques like SMOTE, and extracting new features from existing ones 114.
16. Research Gap: Efficiently addressing massive real-world misclassification cost imbalances in gradient boosting frameworks without relying solely on standard accuracy or F1-scores 101, 109, 115.
17. Explainability Method (if any): None implemented in this study 102-113, 116-121.
18. Concept Drift Handling (if any): None implemented in this study 102-113, 116-121.
19. Industrial Application: Heavy transportation / Commercial automotive (Scania truck manufacturing and maintenance operations) 98, 100.
20. Important Figures/Tables: Table 1 (Cost matrix for binary classification), Table 3 & 4 (Confusion matrices), Table 7 (Metric comparison for equal and unequal class weights), Figure 2 (Class distribution for APS dataset) 103, 107, 109, 121.
Paper 4: Trends and Tracking Topics in Predictive Maintenance Research: A Bibliometric Analysis (2000-2023)
1. Title: Trends and Tracking Topics in Predictive Maintenance Research: A Bibliometric Analysis (2000-2023) 122, 123.
2. Authors: Mohd Firdaus Roslan, Noor Syazwani Ishak, Khairul Hafezad Abdullah, Ashyfah Abdul Hamid, Abdul Muhaimin Mohaidin 122, 124.
3. Year: 2024 122.
4. Research Problem: Due to the rapid growth of publications in predictive maintenance, there is informational overload, and a lack of exploration detailing research trends, specific tracking topics, and under-explored domains 125, 126.
5. Research Objective: To explore publishing patterns, publication trends, and the most commonly used author keywords related to predictive maintenance from Scopus and Web of Science (WoS) databases 123, 125.
6. Methodology: A bibliometric mapping and scientometric analysis utilizing the ScientoPy and VOSviewer software for data preprocessing, duplicate elimination, and analyzing publication trends, country/institution productivity, and keyword co-occurrences 123, 127-130.
7. Dataset(s): A combined dataset of 6,212 processed bibliographic metadata records spanning from 2000 to 2023, sourced from Scopus and the Web of Science (WoS) databases 130, 131.
8. Algorithms: VOSviewer (for co-authorship and co-occurrence networking) and ScientoPy (Python-based script for trend analysis) 127, 128.
9. Features Used: Bibliographic metadata fields including author keywords, publication titles, document types, publication years, source countries, and institutional affiliations 128, 129, 132-134.
10. Evaluation Metrics: Total Publications, Percentage of documents, Average documents per year, co-occurrence cluster formations, and growth rates 132, 135-139.
11. Results: Predictive maintenance literature has experienced significant growth since 2014. Engineering, Computer Science, and Operations Research are the dominant fields. China, the USA, and France are top publishers. Keyword mapping revealed that "predictive maintenance" is most tightly clustered with "machine learning", "data mining", and "monitoring" 123, 133, 139-142.
12. Strengths: Successfully identifies macro-level trends across a large timeframe (23 years) and utilizes rigorous dual-database duplicate-removal processes 129, 131, 143.
13. Weaknesses: The analysis relies purely on bibliographic metadata and does not evaluate the empirical content, methodologies, or findings of the underlying papers 144.
14. Limitations: Findings are restricted to documents indexed in Scopus and WoS; non-indexed literature is excluded 144.
15. Future Work: Suggests conducting systematic literature reviews or meta-analyses to provide deeper evidence synthesis beyond basic keyword metrics 144, 145.
16. Research Gap: Synthesizing the fragmented evolution of multi-disciplinary predictive maintenance literature to highlight core areas, proactive institutions, and productive authors 123, 125.
17. Explainability Method (if any): Not applicable 122-171.
18. Concept Drift Handling (if any): Not applicable 122-171.
19. Industrial Application: Applicable generally across industries utilizing preventive/predictive practices (manufacturing, transportation, utilities) 148.
20. Important Figures/Tables: Figure 1 (Selection strategy and research protocol), Figure 3 (Timeline graph), Figure 9 (Author keywords bar trends), Figure 10 (Network Visualisation of The Co-occurrence), Table 3 (Document type) 129, 132, 139, 140, 161.

Commonalities and Overall Trends
* Common Methodologies: All empirical and review papers emphasize the dominance of Tree-Based Machine Learning Models, particularly XGBoost, Random Forests, and LightGBM, as they effectively handle non-linear tabular data and provide foundations for both incremental learning and feature importance 20, 21, 73, 91, 106. Cost-sensitive learning and automated threshold adjustments are frequently deployed to handle class imbalances 105, 112.
* Common Datasets: Papers commonly rely on sensor, telemetry, or simulation data streams. Examples include the SUMO simulated floating car data, the heavy Scania truck sensor dataset from the UCI repository, and various manufacturing/asset datasets (NASA FD001, Azure telemetry) 16, 69, 71, 100.
* Common Evaluation Metrics: To assess model correctness, standard metrics like Accuracy, F1-Score, RMSE, and MAE are widely used 29, 74, 109. However, operational viability requires specialized metrics like Detection Latency, False Positive Rates, and customized Cost Matrices (e.g., heavily weighting False Negatives to avoid operational breakdowns) 31, 77, 107.
* Common Weaknesses: A recurring weakness across the literature is the heavy reliance on simulated, anonymized, or synthetic datasets, which limits domain-specific interpretations 43, 71, 104. Furthermore, existing techniques often trade off between precision and recall (e.g., prioritizing recall yields high false positives), and post-hoc XAI tools struggle to establish true causal links 82, 109.
* Most frequently mentioned future work: Researchers consistently push for validating models on real-world, operational industrial data streams to escape the confines of synthetic datasets 49, 51, 84. Additionally, there is a strong demand for incorporating predictive uncertainty quantification (e.g., confidence intervals, deep learning uncertainty) to increase end-user trust and refine decision-making in dynamic environments 52, 92, 178.
